# AI_EPISTEMIC_RISK

> **Note:** This document has been updated to reflect the normative rules in [SYSTEM_PATCH_v1](../99_Reference/SYSTEM_PATCH_v1.md) and [SYSTEM_PATCH_v2](../99_Reference/SYSTEM_PATCH_v2.md).


## 1. Purpose

This document defines the epistemic risks introduced by using AI systems in the civic reasoning layer.

Its purpose is to address a danger not solved by ordinary provider diversity:
multiple models, prompts, or vendors may still produce a narrow, shared, and politically consequential way of seeing the world.

This document complements:
- `GOVERNANCE.md`
- `ARCHITECTURE.md`
- `SKILLS.md`
- `SKILL_REGISTRY.md`
- `SKILL_ECONOMICS.md`
- `SKILL_EVALUATION.md`
- `POLITICAL_ECONOMY.md`
- `PACKET_FORMAT.md`
- `CLASSIFICATION.md`
- `PROTOTYPE_PLAN.md`

The problem is not only whether AI is helpful.
The problem is whether the AI layer becomes a hidden epistemic regime.

---

## 2. Core Principle

AI assistance does not become politically harmless merely because humans make the final decision.

If citizens, operators, or communities decide on the basis of:
- AI-generated packets,
- AI-mediated classification,
- AI-generated disagreement maps,
- AI-generated critiques,
- AI-generated summaries of public evidence,

then the AI layer is not peripheral.
It is part of the system's epistemic constitution.

The system must therefore treat the AI layer as:
- useful,
- powerful,
- fallible,
- convergent under pressure,
- and capable of shaping reality through framing long before it makes any formal decision.

---

## 3. What Counts as Epistemic Risk

Epistemic risk is the risk that the system's way of knowing, framing, or surfacing public issues becomes systematically distorted.

This includes risk that the AI layer:
- narrows the space of visible interpretations,
- reproduces dominant assumptions,
- compresses disagreement into legible consensus,
- overweights certain evidence styles,
- underweights minority harms,
- confuses fluency with validity,
- systematically misses certain classes of conflict,
- or creates false confidence in a particular framing of reality.

Epistemic risk is not only factual inaccuracy.
It includes patterned blindness.

---

## 4. Why This Risk Is Distinct

The system already addresses:
- provider concentration,
- operator concentration,
- classification capture,
- funding capture,
- skill dependency,
- treasury power.

AI epistemic risk is distinct because it may persist even when those controls improve.

For example:
- different vendors may still train on overlapping corpora,
- different prompts may still reward similar styles of output,
- different skill roles may still converge on similar abstractions,
- critic loops may still share the same blind spots,
- "plurality" may be nominal while cognition remains structurally homogeneous.

This means:
**provider diversity is not the same as cognitive diversity.**

---

## 5. Main Epistemic Failure Modes

The system should recognize at least the following AI-layer epistemic failure modes.

### 5.1 False Plurality
Multiple skills appear to disagree,
but the disagreement occurs inside the same narrow frame. This includes shared retrieval, shared providers, shared policy layers, and shared training lineage.

### 5.2 Framing Convergence
Different models repeatedly define the problem in similar terms,
excluding alternative political or social interpretations.

### 5.3 Consensus Compression
Real conflict is smoothed into a manageable synthetic middle. Synthesis itself can become an epistemic distortion layer if it averages away real conflict.

### 5.4 Minority Harm Erasure
Diffuse, local, stigmatized, or hard-to-quantify harms are omitted or softened.

### 5.5 Evidence Style Bias
Certain evidence forms are treated as more real because they are more legible to language models.

### 5.6 Fluency Overweighting
Well-phrased reasoning receives more epistemic credibility than weakly phrased but materially important claims.

### 5.7 Proceduralization Bias
Questions of power, class, identity, or politics are reframed as technical process questions.

### 5.8 Safety Flattening
Controversial or adversarial possibilities are softened into generic caution.

### 5.9 Abstract Universalism
Context-specific realities are replaced by general language that feels fair but hides actual distribution.

### 5.10 Packet Monoculture
Different issues begin to look cognitively similar because the packet format trains the AI layer toward one style of legibility.

### 5.11 Fabricated Citations and Source Laundering
The risk of generating plausible but false citations, or laundering weak sources through confident presentation.

### 5.12 Polished False Confidence
The risk of presenting low-evidence claims with high stylistic certainty.

### 5.13 Human Epistemic Risks
Human experts introduce risks such as prestige capture, institutional bias, hidden conflict, and forced consensus.

---

## 6. Sources of Epistemic Monoculture

The system must assume epistemic monoculture may arise from many sources at once.

### 6.1 Shared Training Data
Different models may learn from overlapping public corpora, institutional discourse, and dominant language patterns.

### 6.2 Shared RLHF or Preference Style
Models tuned toward similar notions of "helpful," "balanced," or "safe" output may converge.

### 6.3 Shared Prompt Style
Public skill templates may themselves create monoculture if all skills inherit the same reasoning style.

### 6.4 Shared Evaluation Bias
Benchmarks and task suites may reward certain styles of reasoning while missing alternative but valuable styles.

### 6.5 Shared Context Packets
If all models receive the same curated context, the curation itself can dominate the result.

### 6.6 Shared Translational Norms
The packet format may privilege one mode of explanation and suppress others.

### 6.7 Shared Institutional Inputs
If evidence sources are themselves concentrated, model diversity cannot compensate for source monoculture.

### 6.8 Shared Founding Values Interpretation
The values layer may be interpreted through one founder-informed lens that persists invisibly across models.

---

## 7. Why Human Final Decision Is Not Enough

A human can remain the formal decider while the AI layer remains the effective shaper of the decision surface.

This happens when:
- the packet is the main readable interface to the issue,
- the classification rationale is AI-shaped,
- challenge pathways depend on AI legibility,
- citizens rely on summarized disagreement rather than direct source review,
- the skill layer determines which uncertainties are visible.

In such conditions, human decision remains important but not sufficient protection.

Formal sovereignty can coexist with epistemic dependency.

---

## 8. AI-Layer Power Surfaces

The system must identify the main AI-layer power surfaces.

These include:
- proposal summarization,
- classification assistance,
- panel evidence packets,
- packet synthesis,
- evidence ranking or compression,
- routing rationale generation,
- dissent representation,
- minority-harm surfacing,
- challenge drafting aids,
- audit summarization.

Each of these is an interpretive act.
Interpretive acts are political acts when they shape the public reasoning surface.

---

## 9. False Diversity

The system must distinguish between:
- provider diversity,
- model diversity,
- prompt diversity,
- cognitive diversity,
- genuine executor independence.

These are not the same.

Examples of false diversity:
- multiple skills using the same model family,
- multiple vendors using similar training distributions,
- multiple prompts differing in role language but not epistemic stance,
- critic and generator models sharing the same style of institutional caution,
- panel disagreement that varies conclusion but not framing,
- shared retrieval, shared providers, shared policy layers, and shared training lineage.

A system may look diverse on paper while thinking with one mind. Evidence-backed disagreement is safer than stylistic plurality.

---

## 10. Skill Panels and Epistemic Risk

Plural skill panels reduce some risks but create new illusions.

A five-skill panel can still fail if:
- all skills inherit the same assumptions,
- all skills optimize for the same packet format,
- all skills share the same blind spot toward class or identity conflict,
- all skills overvalue the same evidence style,
- all skills are ultimately critiqued and revised into the same "balanced" tone.

The system must therefore avoid assuming:
**more skills = enough epistemic diversity**

The relevant question is:
**diversity of what?**

---

## 11. Critique Loops Help but Do Not Solve

Critique loops such as:
- apply,
- criticize,
- revise,
- criticize again,
- revise again,

are useful because they:
- expose contradictions,
- pressure certainty,
- surface omissions,
- improve packet shape,
- reduce one-pass tunnel vision.

But critique loops do not automatically produce cognitive plurality.

If all participants in the loop:
- share a training culture,
- share benchmark incentives,
- share a style of "reasonable" output,
- or share the same public context curation,

then the loop may become a highly polished monoculture.

Critique is necessary.
It is not sufficient.

---

## 12. Packet Construction as Epistemic Governance

The packet is one of the main epistemic governance tools of the system.

Packet sections determine what is routinely surfaced as meaningful.

For example:
- strongest case in favor,
- strongest case against,
- unknowns,
- minority view,
- capture-risk note,
- reversibility note,
- implementation note.

This is valuable.
But it also creates a style of public reason.

The system must ask:
- What does this packet structure make easier to see?
- What does it make harder to see?
- Which kinds of politics fit the format?
- Which kinds of politics are flattened by it?

A good packet can still become an epistemic filter.

---

## 13. Evidence and Legibility Bias

AI systems often handle some evidence forms better than others.

More legible to the AI layer:
- textual documentation,
- formal reports,
- structured claims,
- explicit trade-off statements,
- citations with clear wording.

Less legible to the AI layer:
- diffuse social pain,
- local memory,
- tacit norms,
- historically shaped distrust,
- ambiguous or weakly articulated harms,
- community knowledge not written in model-friendly form.

This can create a systematic bias toward what can be easily narrated.

The system must not confuse narrative legibility with public reality.

---

## 14. Minority and Marginalized Epistemics

The system must assume that some groups or harms will be easier for the AI layer to overlook.

This may happen because:
- the source material is sparse,
- the language is outside dominant corpora,
- the grievance is socially discounted,
- the harm is cumulative rather than dramatic,
- the group lacks institutional documentation,
- the experience is better expressed narratively than analytically.

A packet that "looks balanced" may still erase such groups.

Therefore AI-layer evaluation must include:
- minority-harm surfacing checks,
- low-legibility harm checks,
- underrepresented-group omission checks,
- packet challenge routes specific to epistemic omission.

---

## 15. Classification and Epistemic Risk

Classification is not only a protocol lever.
It is an epistemic lever.

If AI assistance helps classify a proposal as:
- trivial,
- policy,
- governance,
- constitutional,
- emergency,

then the AI layer is partly shaping which political world the proposal enters.

Classification bias may arise from:
- underestimating spillover,
- over-normalizing status quo language,
- privileging obvious over structural harms,
- reading abstract wording as non-constitutional when the effects are constitutional,
- treating local or minority concerns as implementation details.

Classification evaluation must therefore include epistemic bias analysis,
not only procedural correctness.

---

## 16. Audit Summaries and Secondary Monoculture

Even the audit layer can become an epistemic bottleneck.

If readable audit views are AI-compressed,
then a second-order monoculture may emerge:
- the packet already frames the issue,
- the audit summary then frames the framing,
- the public ends up reading two layers of the same interpretive style.

The system must therefore preserve:
- canonical traceability,
- drill-down,
- summary-to-source linkage,
- stable exposure of dissent and unknowns,
- visible human and model provenance.

Readable summaries must not become interpretive monopolies.

---

## 17. Evaluation Must Include Epistemic Failure Classes

`SKILL_EVALUATION.md` should be read together with this document.

Evaluation must include failure classes such as:
- `FalsePlurality`
- `FramingConvergence`
- `MinorityHarmErasure`
- `ConsensusCompression`
- `EvidenceStyleBias`
- `ProceduralizationBias`
- `FluencyOverweighting`
- `AbstractUniversalism`
- `ContextFlattening`

A skill may be technically coherent yet epistemically dangerous.

A skill that repeatedly produces "reasonable" but flattening packets is not safe just because it avoids factual hallucination.

---

## 18. Tests for Epistemic Diversity

The system should develop explicit tests for epistemic diversity.

Possible checks include:
- same skill on multiple models,
- same task with different context packings,
- adversarial counter-packets,
- intentionally marginalized-harm test cases,
- high-conflict tasks where procedural flattening is likely,
- comparison of packet framing, not just conclusion,
- tests where the correct output is to preserve irreducible conflict.

The goal is not infinite diversity.
It is resistance to silent sameness.

---

## 19. Cross-Model Use

Using multiple models can help, but only if the system asks what kind of diversity is actually being introduced.

Cross-model use is meaningful when it reveals:
- different uncertainty profiles,
- different omission patterns,
- different evidentiary sensitivities,
- different treatment of local or minority harms,
- different framing tendencies.

Cross-model use is weaker when it merely gives:
- similar packet structures with cosmetic style variation,
- similar centrist smoothing,
- similar classification conservatism,
- similar "safe" language patterns.

Multiple models should be treated as a testable hypothesis, not automatic protection.

---

## 20. Human Review Role

Human review remains necessary,
but human review also has limits.

Humans may share:
- the same institutional background,
- the same class assumptions,
- the same fatigue,
- the same deference to fluent summaries,
- the same desire for manageable outputs.

Therefore "human-in-the-loop" is not a magic answer.

Human review helps when it is:
- plural,
- role-diverse,
- willing to challenge packet framing,
- willing to question what the AI layer made invisible,
- not merely proofreading.

---

## 21. Epistemic Incident Handling

The system should treat epistemic failures as incidents when they are serious enough.

Examples:
- repeated omission of a materially affected group,
- repeated false plurality,
- packet framing that systematically hides power transfer,
- repeated constitutional under-escalation due to framing,
- AI-generated consensus that later proves socially explosive,
- audit summaries that misrepresent dissent.

An epistemic incident should create:
- incident record,
- affected packet list,
- review of root causes,
- mitigation steps,
- evaluation suite updates,
- possible skill demotion or suspension.

---

## 22. Bootstrap Profile

During bootstrap, epistemic risk is often higher because:
- model diversity is thinner,
- provider dependence is higher,
- evaluation suites are weaker,
- founder framing is stronger,
- community challenge capacity is weaker,
- packets may be over-trusted because the tool is novel.

Bootstrap should therefore:
- label skill tier and model dependence clearly,
- prefer visible prompts and scaffolds where possible,
- keep packet challenge simple,
- test same issues across more than one model where affordable,
- maintain epistemic incident logs,
- avoid overclaiming "plurality" early.

Bootstrap must not mistake multi-step prompting for solved epistemic governance.

---

## 23. Signs of Epistemic Monoculture

The following are warning signs:

- different models repeatedly produce the same framing,
- packets always converge on a neat middle,
- low-legibility harms disappear unless manually inserted,
- challenges mainly catch omissions the skill layer should have caught,
- provider diversity increases but packet style remains unchanged,
- all skills sound adversarial but reach the same abstraction layer,
- "minority view" becomes stylistically present but substantively weak,
- politically charged issues become depoliticized into management language,
- local context is consistently flattened into universal abstractions.

Repeated signs require redesign, not just more model calls.

---

## 24. Mitigation Directions

The system should consider the following mitigations:

- explicit epistemic diversity tests,
- stronger minority-harm and low-legibility-harm tasks,
- packet sections that expose framing limits,
- adversarial skills designed to question the packet frame itself,
- model-family disclosure in public packets where relevant,
- challenge routes specifically for omitted perspective claims,
- human reviewers selected for perspective diversity, not only procedural role,
- deliberate comparison between packet versions produced under different epistemic assumptions,
- incident-driven evaluation updates,
- ranking and revalidation as anti-epistemic-fragility mechanisms.

The goal is not perfect pluralism.
It is visible resistance to hidden sameness.

### 24.1 Reuse-Status Labeling

A specific mitigation for the epistemic feedback loop (models → artifacts → future models)
is the reuse-status labeling defined in `../90_Information/SCHEMAS.md` §3.9:

- `statement_status`: verified / corrected / disproven / unresolved,
- prediction vs observed outcome,
- simulated vs actual participation.

Artifacts intended for reuse carry these labels so a future reader — human or model —
can distinguish what was claimed from what happened,
and cannot ingest a disproven statement or a simulated exchange as settled civic reality.

Two invariants anchor this (`../80_Runtime/INVARIANTS.md`):
a simulated citizen population is never public participation,
and raw hidden model reasoning is never canonical public provenance.

---

## 25. What the System Must Never Assume

The system must never assume that:
- more models automatically means more perspectives,
- more skills automatically means more plurality,
- "balanced tone" means balanced epistemics,
- challenge pathways alone solve framing power,
- human final review neutralizes AI-layer influence,
- vendor diversity equals cognitive diversity,
- packet readability is neutral,
- evaluation benchmarks automatically capture political blind spots.

Any design that assumes these things has already under-modeled AI epistemic risk.

## 27. Closing Principle

The greatest danger of the AI layer is not that it will always be obviously wrong.

The greater danger is that it may become calmly, fluently, procedurally, and repeatedly narrow in the same direction.

A democratic system should therefore fear not only AI error,
but AI sameness.

The task is not to eliminate AI assistance.
It is to ensure that the public reasoning surface does not become a polished monoculture wearing the mask of plural deliberation.
