# FUNDING_MODEL

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the funding model of the system.

Its purpose is not merely to describe how money enters and exits a treasury.
Its purpose is to define how funding:
- sustains public reasoning capacity,
- finances civic infrastructure,
- supports skill diversity,
- protects challenge capacity,
- enables local experimentation,
- reduces dependence on existing power centers,
- and creates real organizational leverage against entrenched political and administrative structures.

This document complements:
- `GOVERNANCE.md`
- `ARCHITECTURE.md`
- `POLITICAL_ECONOMY.md`
- `SKILL_ECONOMICS.md`
- `MINIMUM_VIABLE_PNYX.md`
- `AUDIT_LOG.md`
- `AUDIT_VIEWS.md`

Funding must be treated as a constitutional lever.
It is not neutral plumbing.

---

## 2. Core Principle

Money does not confer legitimacy.

Funding may build:
- capacity,
- continuity,
- infrastructure,
- challenge pathways,
- translation layers,
- legal resilience,
- local pilot capability,
- independence from incumbents.

But funding alone may not buy:
- constitutional validity,
- registry admission,
- routing outcomes,
- proposal success,
- emergency privilege,
- interpretive authority,
- public trust by default.

The system must therefore distinguish:
- **funding power**,
- **governance power**,
- **constitutional legitimacy**.

These may interact.
They must not collapse into each other.

---

## 3. Why Funding Matters

A public system without independent funding is easily domesticated.

Without a funding model, the system risks dependence on:
- existing political parties,
- state ministries,
- incumbent bureaucracies,
- dominant NGOs,
- platform companies,
- model vendors,
- philanthropic gatekeepers,
- wealthy patrons.

Funding is therefore not only a support function.
It is part of the anti-capture architecture.

The system must be able to finance at least:
- core operations,
- auditability,
- challenge and appeals,
- skill commons,
- translation and access,
- local pilots,
- incident response,
- migration and replacement,
- legal defense where appropriate.

---

## 4. Funding Objectives

The funding model should aim to produce:

1. **Operational continuity**
2. **Political independence**
3. **Plural support base**
4. **Transparent treasury flows**
5. **Low capture susceptibility**
6. **Ability to fund challengers, not only operators**
7. **Ability to support local experiments**
8. **Capacity to exert civic pressure on existing institutions**
9. **Ability to survive withdrawal by any single funder**

A healthy funding model must make the system harder to starve, not easier to buy.

---

## 5. Sources of Funds

The system may accept multiple funding sources, provided each is disclosed and governed.

Possible sources include:
- small recurring member contributions,
- one-time public donations,
- crowdfunding campaigns,
- earmarked civic funds,
- local pooled budgets,
- public-interest grants,
- cooperative membership dues,
- service revenues from optional non-sovereign tools,
- challenge bounties,
- public matching mechanisms,
- treasury yields under strict limits where legally appropriate.

**Prohibited source — personal civic data.**
Personal civic data may never be a funding source.
The system may not fund itself through sale, behavioral exploitation, or undisclosed commercial use of personal civic data, directly or through intermediaries.
This is an economic invariant (`../80_Runtime/INVARIANTS.md` §13.1), not a revocable policy choice.

Each source must be assessed for:
- concentration risk,
- conditionality risk,
- reputational risk,
- legal dependence,
- renewal fragility,
- influence potential.

No funding source is politically innocent merely because it is lawful.

---

## 6. Funding Tiers

The system should distinguish funding tiers by constitutional sensitivity.

### 6.1 Core Constitutional Infrastructure
Funds for:
- audit systems,
- packet publication,
- core protocol maintenance,
- public visibility surfaces,
- challenge pathways,
- registry and classification infrastructure,
- emergency containment infrastructure.

This tier requires the strongest anti-capture constraints.

### 6.2 Skill and Evaluation Infrastructure
Funds for:
- skill commons,
- evaluation suites,
- benchmark commons,
- challenger capacity,
- translation and accessibility layers,
- minority-interest tools,
- model portability work.

### 6.3 Local Civic Experimentation
Funds for:
- pilots,
- local deliberation support,
- local issue packs,
- participatory studies,
- community interfaces,
- civic education aids.

### 6.4 Auxiliary and Optional Services
Funds for non-core tools, optional services, research sandboxes, and experimental layers.
These must not become hidden gateways into governance power.

Different tiers require different scrutiny, treasury rules, and donor influence limits.

---

## 7. Treasury Structure

The funding model should maintain at least the following treasury partitions:

- `CoreTreasury`
- `ChallengeTreasury`
- `SkillCommonsTreasury`
- `LocalPilotTreasury`
- `EmergencyReserve`
- `MigrationAndExitReserve`

Each partition should have:
- stated purpose,
- eligible uses,
- release rules,
- disclosure level,
- concentration thresholds,
- reserve targets where relevant.

Partitioning prevents one funding stream from silently dominating the whole system.

---

## 8. Core Funding Logic

The default funding posture should prefer:
- many small contributors over few large patrons,
- stable recurring contributions over erratic bursts,
- public disclosures over opaque sponsorship,
- general support over tightly conditional influence,
- challenge capacity over operator comfort,
- reserves over brittle dependency,
- portability over short-term subsidy traps.

The model should not chase the largest available money if that money creates constitutional dependency.

---

## 9. Member Contributions

Small recurring contributions may provide the healthiest default base because they:
- distribute support,
- reduce donor concentration,
- create sustained civic attachment,
- make treasury legitimacy more socially rooted,
- create an autonomous funding floor.

Member contribution systems should avoid:
- pay-to-govern dynamics,
- contribution-based voting weight,
- access privilege for donors,
- hidden elite clubs.

Contribution may build the commons.
It must not purchase sovereignty.

---

## 10. Crowdfunding

Crowdfunding is a legitimate mechanism within the funding model,
but it must be constitutionally bounded.

Crowdfunding is useful for:
- local pilots,
- accessibility tools,
- watchdog and challenge tooling,
- translation,
- skill commons growth,
- public campaigns around clearly scoped infrastructure,
- emergency civic recovery needs,
- public legal defense funds,
- issue-specific mobilization.

Crowdfunding is risky because it may amplify:
- wealthy-donor influence,
- salience bias,
- emotional surges,
- identity mobilization,
- media-driven distortion,
- attention inequality.

Therefore crowdfunding must disclose:
- campaign purpose,
- target amount,
- donor concentration,
- donor caps if used,
- matching logic if used,
- whether the funds create commons or privileged service,
- whether the funded asset becomes public infrastructure,
- whether the campaign affects registry or governance status.

Crowdfunding may finance capacity.
It may not buy legitimacy.

---

## 11. Matching and Equalization

To reduce concentration effects, the system may use matching mechanisms.

Possible methods include:
- capped matching for small donations,
- quadratic-style matching,
- local-equalization pools,
- underrepresented-community boosts,
- challenge-path matching,
- commons-support multipliers.

The purpose of matching is not mathematical elegance.
It is to push the funding surface away from domination by actors with the deepest pockets.

Matching rules must be:
- public,
- stable,
- auditable,
- prospectively applied,
- challengeable.

No matching formula may silently redirect funds for political convenience.

---

## 12. Donor Limits and Concentration Controls

A healthy system should limit funding concentration, especially in core tiers.

Possible controls include:
- single-donor caps,
- source-class caps,
- campaign concentration warnings,
- mandatory cooling review above thresholds,
- treasury partition caps,
- no-default rights from large contributions,
- enhanced scrutiny for bundled donations.

The system must distinguish:
- legal permissibility,
- constitutional prudence.

A lawful donation may still create unacceptable dependency.

---

## 13. Conditional Funding Rules

Some funding arrives with conditions.

Conditions may concern:
- timeline,
- deliverables,
- public reporting,
- class coverage,
- language support,
- open licensing,
- benchmark publication.

Conditions may **not** concern:
- routing outcomes,
- default skill status,
- panel composition,
- classification outcomes,
- challenge suppression,
- packet wording,
- emergency privileges,
- constitutional interpretation.

Any funding condition touching governance power is invalid.

---

## 14. What Funding May Support

Funding may support:
- commons and infrastructure,
- challenge and watchdog capacity,
- skill development and evaluation,
- local pilots,
- migration and exit work,
- access and translation,
- legal defense tied to system integrity,
- audit visibility tooling,
- civic education and onboarding,
- research on participation inequality.

Funding should prefer public returns where public money or public donations are involved.

---

## 15. What Funding May Not Buy

Funding may not buy:
- votes,
- routing priority,
- classification downgrades,
- meta-governance shortcuts,
- default registry admission,
- permanent operator roles,
- immunity from challenge,
- ability to suppress audit visibility,
- exclusive access to constitutional interpretation.

And funding may never come from monetizing personal civic data (section 5; `../80_Runtime/INVARIANTS.md` §13.1).

Any system that allows money to buy these things is no longer civic governance.
It is rent-seeking with better prose.

---

## 16. Funding as Civic Leverage

Funding is also a political instrument.

If the system can raise and allocate money independently,
it gains the ability to:
- support local pilots outside party structures,
- finance evidence gathering where incumbents refuse to act,
- sustain watchdog and adversarial skill layers,
- build parallel civic competence,
- fund translation and inclusion where the state neglects them,
- support legal and organizational resistance to procedural exclusion,
- demonstrate demand with tangible commitment,
- coordinate communities around concrete public works.

This creates pressure on the existing establishment because it changes the question from:
- “Will incumbents permit this?”
to:
- “Can incumbents ignore a funded, organized, auditable civic capacity?”

Funding, when organized correctly, becomes a lever of civic reality.

---

## 17. Pressure on the Existing Establishment

The system should understand its funding model as a pressure mechanism in at least five ways.

### 17.1 Proof of Demand
Many small contributors demonstrate that the project is not merely a founder fantasy.
It has social backing.

### 17.2 Independent Capacity
A funded civic system can produce packets, audits, challenges, pilots, and public tools without waiting for party or ministry approval.

### 17.3 Public Comparison
Local pilots and public outputs create direct comparisons with existing institutions.
This pressures incumbents through visible competence.

### 17.4 Institutional Bargaining Power
If the system can finance its own evaluations, challenge mechanisms, and local coordination, it negotiates with incumbents from less dependency.

### 17.5 Exit Pressure
Even when it does not fully replace incumbent structures,
a self-financing civic layer creates a credible threat of parallel legitimacy and parallel organization.

This does not mean money replaces politics.
It means money can finance the organizational substrate through which politics becomes harder to ignore.

---

## 18. Public Treasury Ethic

The treasury must be run according to a public ethic.

That ethic should include:
- no silent reallocation,
- no hidden sponsorship,
- no donor privilege in governance,
- no treasury use outside declared tiers,
- no starving the challenge side to fatten the operator side,
- no emergency spending without incident traceability,
- no dependence on one “friendly” patron treated as stable destiny.

Treasury conduct is constitutional conduct.

---

## 19. Allocation Models

Treasury allocation may use multiple models depending on tier.

Possible models include:
- baseline budget allocation,
- campaign-targeted funding,
- milestone release,
- matching pools,
- challenge-triggered release,
- reserve-based emergency release,
- local participatory budgeting,
- registry and evaluation grants,
- adversarial bounty funding.

Allocation logic must be:
- explicit,
- versioned,
- auditable,
- challengeable,
- bound to purpose.

## 20. Funding and Skills

The funding model must integrate with `SKILL_ECONOMICS.md`.

Funding should distinguish:
- commons growth,
- evaluation hardening,
- governance-grade maintenance,
- challenger skill support,
- portability work,
- open benchmark maintenance.

The system must not finance only the skills that make operators comfortable.
It must also finance the skills that criticize, challenge, and replace them.

A healthy funding model pays for contestability.

---

## 21. Funding and Participation Inequality

Funding should be used to reduce participation inequality where possible.

This may include:
- translation,
- local facilitation,
- accessibility tooling,
- public summaries,
- travel or access stipends where lawful and appropriate,
- community-hosted deliberation surfaces,
- support for materially affected but underrepresented groups,
- low-friction challenge access.

If all funds go to core operators and none to access,
formal openness will hide substantive exclusion.

### 21.1 Participant Compensation Firewall

Where participants are compensated for civic participation time
(`ParticipantCompensation`, `TREASURY.md` §12; `45_Participation/PARTICIPATION_MODEL.md`),
the following rules are mandatory:

- compensation terms must be declared before participation begins,
- compensation must be equal or transparently differentiated,
- compensation must be independent of the participant's position, vote, or agreement,
- compensation must be independent of the final decision.

Compensation pays for time and civic work.
Compensation that varies with how a participant decided is vote buying,
whatever the paperwork calls it.

---

## 22. Emergency and Reserve Logic

The model should maintain reserves for:
- infrastructure compromise,
- legal defense tied to system integrity,
- urgent migration,
- challenge continuity during crisis,
- identity and verification continuity,
- public visibility continuity.

Emergency reserves must not become operator discretionary pools.
They remain bound to:
- defined emergency classes,
- incident traceability,
- post-emergency review,
- restoration requirements.

---

## 23. Funding Transparency

Funding transparency should include at least:
- source category,
- amount or band,
- treasury partition,
- restrictions if any,
- donor concentration indicators,
- campaign summaries,
- release decisions,
- matching logic,
- unresolved funding conflicts.

Where privacy is necessary for small donors, structured disclosure may be used.
But opacity must not hide concentration.

---

## 24. Audit and Public Views

Funding must be visible through readable and canonical audit surfaces.

At minimum, the public should be able to see:
- where money came from,
- what treasury partition received it,
- what it funded,
- whether concentration thresholds were crossed,
- whether the allocation changed operator or challenger capacity,
- whether a campaign created public infrastructure or private benefit.

Funding records must remain linked to canonical audit traces.

---

## 25. Governance Boundaries

Funding decisions and governance decisions must remain distinct, even when connected.

Funding may:
- enable a pilot,
- enable a challenge,
- enable a benchmark,
- enable an infrastructure improvement.

Funding may not:
- predetermine the civic result of the pilot,
- predetermine the classification outcome,
- predetermine the route,
- predetermine constitutional acceptance.

The treasury may support civic action.
It may not silently govern in the treasury’s name.

---

## 26. Bootstrap Funding Profile

Bootstrap should favor:
- broad small-donor support,
- narrow clearly scoped campaigns,
- transparent dependence declarations,
- strong reserve discipline,
- low fixed burn,
- support for challenge capacity from the start,
- local experimentation over institution-scale expansion,
- explicit disclosure of any major donor dependence.

Bootstrap should avoid:
- founder myth financed by one patron,
- expensive permanent staff before civic demand is proven,
- core dependence on one grant cycle,
- invisible subsidy from a model vendor or platform,
- confusing fundraising success with constitutional success.

Bootstrap funding is not proof of legitimacy.
It is proof of capacity.

---

## 27. Funding Failure Signals

The following indicate likely capture or instability:

- one donor dominates a core treasury partition,
- challenge capacity remains underfunded while operator comfort expands,
- crowdfunding repeatedly follows media drama rather than public need,
- registry or packet influence tracks donor concentration,
- hidden platform or vendor subsidy appears structurally necessary,
- local pilots cannot survive without one patron,
- reserves are consumed by routine operations,
- conditional funding begins to shape governance behavior,
- funding transparency is good enough for PR but weak for real scrutiny,
- the system cannot survive withdrawal of one major source,
- participant compensation correlates abnormally with participant positions or votes.

Repeated failure signals require redesign, not just better fundraising.

## 29. Closing Principle

A civic system that cannot fund itself cannot remain politically independent for long.

But a civic system that lets money quietly become governance has already surrendered.

The correct funding model therefore does two things at once:
- it builds enough autonomous material capacity to resist domestication,
- and it prevents treasury power from masquerading as legitimacy.

Money must build the civic substrate.
It must never become the hidden sovereign.
