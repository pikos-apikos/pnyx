# POLITICAL_ECONOMY

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the political-economy assumptions of the system.

Its purpose is to prevent the fiction that procedural clarity alone is enough to produce legitimate collective self-government.
The system must assume from the start that:
- participants enter with unequal resources,
- institutions are shaped by material incentives,
- information flows are power flows,
- distributional conflict is normal,
- market and state pathways are not neutral containers,
- technical mediation can reproduce elite control unless explicitly constrained.

This document therefore complements:
- `GOVERNANCE.md`,
- `ARCHITECTURE.md`,
- `ROUTING.md`,
- `SKILL_REGISTRY.md`,
- `CLASSIFICATION.md`,
- `MINIMUM_VIABLE_PNYX.md`.

It does not replace procedural rules.
It defines the social and economic conditions under which procedural rules must be interpreted.

---

## 2. Core Premise

Politics cannot be fully proceduralized away.

A well-formed civic loop may improve visibility, reduce arbitrary opacity, and constrain certain forms of capture.
It does not eliminate:
- class conflict,
- identity conflict,
- resource competition,
- organizational asymmetry,
- media influence,
- time inequality,
- institutional inertia,
- dependency on infrastructure providers.

The system must therefore treat governance not only as a problem of process design,
but as a problem of power distribution.

---

## 3. Political-Economy Baseline

The system assumes the following baseline conditions:

### 3.1 Unequal Capacity
Not all citizens have equal time, literacy, technical competence, emotional bandwidth, or institutional confidence.

### 3.2 Unequal Voice
Some actors possess larger audiences, stronger brands, superior organizing capacity, or privileged access to communication channels.

### 3.3 Unequal Stakes
The impact of the same proposal may differ radically across groups, classes, regions, and institutional positions.

### 3.4 Unequal Adaptation Speed
Well-resourced actors adapt to procedural systems faster than ordinary participants.

### 3.5 Unequal Strategic Competence
Professional organizations can game thresholds, timing, classification, evidence framing, and public narrative more effectively than individuals.

### 3.6 Unequal Infrastructure Control
Compute, model supply, cloud infrastructure, security operations, identity systems, payment rails, and media surfaces tend toward concentration.

These are not edge cases.
They are default conditions.

---

## 4. Formal Equality Is Not Sufficient

The system may provide equal formal standing without thereby producing equal practical agency.

Equal eligibility does not guarantee:
- equal ability to understand proposals,
- equal ability to challenge classifications,
- equal ability to detect manipulative framing,
- equal ability to follow audit trails,
- equal ability to organize counter-coalitions,
- equal ability to survive long decision cycles.

The system must therefore distinguish:
- **formal equality**: equal status under the rules,
- **substantive political capacity**: realistic ability to use the rules.

The presence of the first does not imply the presence of the second.

---

## 5. Distributional Conflict

Many political questions are not merely informational disagreements.
They are conflicts over:
- money,
- risk exposure,
- labor conditions,
- land use,
- taxation,
- service access,
- regulatory burden,
- ownership,
- institutional discretion,
- future bargaining power.

A civic packet may clarify the dispute.
It cannot convert a distributional conflict into a purely epistemic one.

The system must therefore record, when relevant:
- who bears the cost,
- who captures the upside,
- who gains discretion,
- who loses fallback options,
- who becomes dependent,
- who exits the risk surface while others remain exposed.

Where distributional conflict is material, the packet must say so directly.

---

## 6. Group Power and Identity

Participants do not enter public reasoning as abstract civic atoms.
They also enter as members of:
- classes,
- professions,
- unions,
- firms,
- neighborhoods,
- cultural communities,
- political factions,
- identity groups,
- administrative strata.

The system must not reduce these realities to noise.

This does **not** mean the system should freeze politics into permanent group essentialism.
It means:
- interests are socially organized,
- perceptions are group-mediated,
- trust is unevenly distributed,
- legitimacy is partly relational.

Packets, audits, and routing rationales must therefore be designed with the assumption that different groups will read the same proposal differently for reasons that are not irrational.

---

## 7. Participation Inequality

Open participation does not guarantee balanced participation.

Likely overrepresented participants include:
- highly educated actors,
- professional advocates,
- time-rich citizens,
- strong writers,
- organizational insiders,
- technical users,
- actors with direct financial stake.

Likely underrepresented participants include:
- shift workers,
- caregivers,
- low-trust populations,
- low-literacy participants,
- people with limited digital access,
- politically fatigued populations,
- groups historically excluded from institutions.

The system must assume this inequality is persistent.

Procedural openness without countermeasures can become participation laundering:
the appearance of openness masking systematic skew.

---

## 8. Attention as Scarce Resource

Attention is a political resource.

Actors able to:
- set agenda,
- frame urgency,
- flood channels,
- generate noise,
- manufacture controversy,
- delay review,
- overwhelm challenge pathways,

possess real political leverage even when they hold no formal office.

The system must therefore treat:
- timing,
- packet volume,
- notification burden,
- challenge complexity,
- review windows,
- issue bundling,

as political-economy variables, not merely UX details.

---

## 9. Expertise as Power

Expertise is not neutral merely because it is useful.

In this system, expertise may appear through:
- human institutions,
- public officials,
- subject-matter bodies,
- AI skills,
- registry structures,
- operators,
- auditors,
- cryptographic verifiers.

Each of these can become a choke point.

The system must assume:
- expertise can be genuine and still politically consequential,
- translation into “accessible civic form” is an exercise of interpretive power,
- citizens may become dependent on those who summarize, classify, and explain.

Therefore:
- skills must be plural,
- packet sections must preserve dissent and unknowns,
- evidence framing must remain challengeable,
- no single interpreter may become default sovereign mediator.

---

## 10. Media and Narrative Power

Public judgment does not occur in an informational vacuum.

External media ecosystems shape:
- salience,
- legitimacy,
- emotional intensity,
- partisan framing,
- scapegoating,
- perceived urgency,
- trust in institutions.

The system cannot fully control this.

It must therefore avoid assuming that better internal procedure automatically produces better external understanding.

At minimum, the system must:
- keep canonical public records stable,
- expose manipulative compression or misquotation,
- support direct citation back to packet and audit material,
- avoid relying on charisma or spokesperson monopoly.

---

## 11. Skill Supply as Political Economy

AI skills are not just technical components.
They are products of capital, infrastructure, labor, and institutional trust.

The system must assume:
- model development tends toward concentration,
- frontier training requires large compute budgets,
- governance-grade evaluation is expensive,
- vendor branding influences perceived legitimacy,
- dependency can form long before monopoly is explicit.

Accordingly:
- the registry is public infrastructure, not a convenience list,
- provider diversity is a political requirement, not cosmetic variety,
- open templates and public benchmarks are anti-capture tools,
- single-provider dependence must be recorded as constitutional debt.

The goal is not to eliminate dependence instantly.
The goal is to prevent dependence from becoming invisible or permanent.

---

## 12. Market and State as Power Structures

Market and state are not merely execution channels.
They are historically loaded arrangements of power.

### 12.1 Market Path Risks
A market path may increase:
- exclusion by price,
- provider lock-in,
- uneven service quality,
- regional disparity,
- opacity through contracts,
- public dependence on private infrastructure.

### 12.2 State Path Risks
A state path may increase:
- bureaucratic concentration,
- rigid universalism,
- enforcement opacity,
- political patronage,
- administrative inertia,
- difficulty of exit.

### 12.3 Hybrid Path Risks
Hybrid arrangements may:
- privatize upside,
- socialize downside,
- diffuse accountability,
- blur responsibility,
- multiply veto points.

Routing must therefore never present market/state/hybrid choices as value-neutral optimization.
Each route is a political distribution of discretion, accountability, and dependence.

---

## 13. Routing and Distributional Disclosure

Where routing is material, the system must disclose:
- who gains implementation power,
- who bears compliance burden,
- whether the route creates dependency chokepoints,
- whether the route centralizes interpretive authority,
- whether the route fragments universal guarantees,
- whether the route shifts costs into hidden private spaces,
- whether the route increases or reduces exit options.

Routing records must not hide behind technical language.
If a route transfers power, the packet must say so.

---

## 14. Classification as Material Leverage

Classification is not only a procedural lever.
It is also a material lever.

To classify a proposal as:
- trivial,
- ordinary policy,
- governance,
- constitutional,
- emergency,

is to shape:
- scrutiny,
- time,
- who can intervene,
- cost of challenge,
- practical outcome probability.

Therefore classification disputes are not clerical disagreements.
They are power disputes over institutional friction.

The system must treat repeated downward classification pressure as a political-economy signal, not merely a protocol anomaly.

---

## 15. Time, Friction, and Delay

Delay is not neutral.

Some actors benefit from:
- fast decisions,
- emergency framing,
- fatigue,
- review bottlenecks,
- endless reconsideration,
- procedural exhaustion,
- challenge overload.

Others benefit from:
- more time,
- more notice,
- more translation,
- more local consultation,
- more audit visibility.

The system must therefore ask, for every major procedural parameter:
- who benefits from speed?
- who benefits from slowness?
- who can afford prolonged participation?
- who is excluded by urgency?

---

## 16. Cost of Challenge

A right to challenge is not meaningful if the cost of exercising it is too high.

Challenge cost may take the form of:
- technical complexity,
- time burden,
- reputational risk,
- social retaliation,
- financial cost,
- documentation burden,
- procedural confusion.

The system must monitor when challenge rights exist in theory but are practically monopolized by experts or insiders.

A healthy system does not merely log challenges.
It makes challenge pathways usable.

---

## 17. Civic Packets Are Interpretive Power

Civic packets are useful.
They are also acts of compression.

Compression can distort by:
- hiding minority harms,
- overstating certainty,
- smoothing value conflict,
- muting class effects,
- depoliticizing routing choices,
- privileging legible interests over diffuse harms.

Therefore packet design must include:
- explicit distributional section,
- explicit “who benefits / who bears cost” section,
- explicit unknowns,
- dissent preservation,
- routing power analysis,
- reversibility note.

Readable form must not become legitimacy theater.

---

## 18. Auditability Is Not the Same as Accessibility

A system may be fully auditable and still inaccessible to most people.

The system must distinguish:
- **canonical truth surface**: what is preserved,
- **public comprehension surface**: what is realistically understandable.

Both matter.

If only experts can navigate the audit surface,
interpretive elites reappear.

This does not invalidate audit.
It means public observability must be treated as a democratic capability, not a logging feature.

---

## 19. Bootstrap Realism

During bootstrap, the political-economy risks are sharper, not weaker.

Likely conditions include:
- a small founder set,
- overlapping roles,
- thin provider diversity,
- weak external oversight,
- fragile civic legitimacy,
- strong dependence on a few operators,
- low procedural literacy.

The system must therefore refuse bootstrap mythology.

Bootstrap must be described honestly as:
- legitimacy-limited,
- capacity-constrained,
- debt-bearing,
- temporary,
- review-bound.

No bootstrap design should pretend to have solved concentration merely by naming it.

---

## 20. Minimum Political-Economy Commitments for Bootstrap

At minimum, bootstrap must include:

1. **Distributional disclosure**
   - every non-trivial proposal states who benefits, who pays, who gains leverage.

2. **Participation inequality note**
   - packets note whether the affected parties are likely underrepresented.

3. **Provider dependence disclosure**
   - single-provider or thin-provider dependence is declared.

4. **Role concentration disclosure**
   - overlapping founder/operator/registry/verifier roles are public.

5. **No silent urgency**
   - emergency or fast-path framing must include who benefits from compressed time.

6. **Challenge usability**
   - at least one low-friction challenge path must exist.

7. **Material routing disclosure**
   - route choice must state power transfer effects.

These are the minimum political-economy obligations of a bootstrap system.

---

## 21. What the System Must Never Assume

The system must never assume that:
- citizens are primarily neutral deliberators,
- expertise is politically innocent,
- procedural openness equals inclusion,
- market and state are interchangeable containers,
- auditability automatically creates trust,
- value conflict can be dissolved into evidence conflict,
- technical mediation is neutral,
- complexity is self-justifying.

Any design that assumes these things is already captured by abstraction.

## 23. Failure Signals

The following are political-economy failure signals:

- proposals repeatedly framed as technical when distributional stakes are high,
- challenge pathways used mainly by experts and insiders,
- public packets that understate who bears costs,
- routing records that hide power transfer behind implementation language,
- concentrated provider dependence without retirement path,
- high formal participation with narrow social composition,
- repeated urgency benefiting already powerful actors,
- summary layers trusted more than underlying traceability,
- stable underrepresentation of materially affected groups.

Repeated failure signals require not only audit, but redesign.

---

## 24. Closing Principle

A democratic system fails when it mistakes visible procedure for balanced power.

This system must therefore not ask only:
- Was the process followed?
- Was the packet published?
- Was the evidence cited?
- Was the audit complete?

It must also ask:
- Who had time to participate?
- Who controlled interpretation?
- Who gained leverage?
- Who paid the cost?
- Who became dependent?
- Who was formally included but substantively absent?

A civic machine that cannot ask these questions will reproduce the inequalities it claims to civilize.
