# GOVERNANCE

> **Note:** This document has been updated to reflect the normative rules in [SYSTEM_PATCH_v1](../99_Reference/SYSTEM_PATCH_v1.md) and [SYSTEM_PATCH_v2](../99_Reference/SYSTEM_PATCH_v2.md).


## 1. Purpose

This system exists to make collective self-government possible under conditions of complexity, scale, and cognitive overload.

Its purpose is:
- to preserve human sovereignty,
- to reduce the knowledge burden required for meaningful participation,
- to prevent capture by concentrated power,
- to keep public reasoning visible, revisable, and auditable.

The system does not replace citizens with artificial intelligence.
It equips citizens with structured access to expertise.

---

## 2. Sovereign Principle

The citizen remains the final source of political legitimacy.

AI systems, institutional processes, and execution channels may assist, filter, simulate, critique, or implement, but they do not become the sovereign subject.

Human judgment is primary.
Machine reasoning is instrumental.

---

## 3. Civic Identity

Any participant entering the system enters first as a civic subject, not as a consumer, client, or managed population unit.

Participation implies:
- equal civic standing,
- access to the same public reasoning surface,
- protection from coercion, opacity, and procedural exclusion,
- obligation to deliberate within the shared constitutional frame.

---

## 4. Shared Values Layer

All governance rests on a common values layer.
This layer is prior to execution and binds every institutional path.

Core values include:
- liberty,
- dignity,
- anti-capture,
- transparency,
- public auditability,
- pluralism,
- reversibility,
- subsidiarity where possible,
- collective protection where necessary.

No proposal is evaluated outside this layer.
The values layer holds the wheel.

---

## 5. Layers of Governance

The system distinguishes three levels of decision:

### 5.1 Policy Layer
Ordinary public decisions about concrete matters, services, rules, priorities, and allocations.

### 5.2 Governance Layer
Changes to procedures, thresholds, routing logic, deliberation mechanisms, and institutional operations.

### 5.3 Constitutional Layer
Changes to shared values, rights guarantees, anti-capture safeguards, and core legitimacy rules.

The deeper the layer, the stricter the process.

---

## 6. The Civic Reasoning Loop

Every proposal passes through a common civic loop before public decision.

The loop includes:
1. proposal intake,
2. scope clarification,
3. assignment to relevant public reasoning tools,
4. structured adversarial review,
5. public briefing generation,
6. deliberation,
7. decision,
8. audit and post-decision review.

The loop exists to transform raw proposals into civic-grade judgment objects.

---

## 7. Role of AI Skills

Specialized AI skills act as public reasoning instruments.

Their function is to:
- translate complexity into accessible civic form,
- expose trade-offs,
- identify implementation constraints,
- surface rights implications,
- produce structured disagreement,
- detect capture risk,
- help route proposals toward proper execution channels.

They do not vote.
They do not rule.
They do not possess legitimacy independent of the public process.

Their outputs must remain inspectable, challengeable, and replaceable.

---

## 8. Public Skill Panel

Each non-trivial proposal is examined by a panel of relevant skills.

The system should require a minimum panel of five relevant skills for any non-trivial proposal.
Five is the minimum deliberative quorum: enough to produce structured plurality, not enough to create excessive coordination drag.

A baseline panel should normally include:
- rights / constitutional skill,
- implementation / feasibility skill,
- economic / resource skill,
- anti-capture / audit skill,
- adversarial critique skill.

Additional skills may be added where the subject demands it.
The system may escalate to seven or more skills for high-impact, high-uncertainty, or cross-domain proposals.

Disagreement is a feature, not a defect.

---

## 9. Structured Disagreement

The system must not compress disagreement into false consensus.

Every civic briefing should show:
- strongest case in favor,
- strongest case against,
- unknowns,
- minority view,
- capture-risk note,
- reversibility note,
- implementation note.

Public judgment should emerge from clarified conflict, not hidden conflict.

---

## 10. Execution Threads: Market and State

The system recognizes two primary execution channels:

### 10.1 Market Thread
Used where experimentation, decentralization, local initiative, and voluntary coordination can produce better outcomes.

### 10.2 State Thread
Used where universality, rights protection, baseline guarantees, coordination, enforcement, or anti-capture intervention are necessary.

These are not rival ideologies.
They are execution pathways operating under one shared moral layer.

A proposal may be routed to:
- market execution,
- state execution,
- hybrid execution.

Routing must be explicitly justified.

---

## 11. Routing Logic

Routing decisions should answer:
- Can this be safely decentralized?
- Does this create exclusion if left voluntary?
- Does this require universal protection?
- Does this create a choke point?
- Does private control produce public dependency?
- Does state control produce concentration risk?

The question is never “market or state by dogma.”
The question is “which path best serves the shared values under present conditions?”

---

## 12. Anti-Capture Doctrine

Anti-capture is a standing requirement across all layers.

Every proposal must be reviewed for:
- disproportionate benefit to a single actor,
- creation of dependency chokepoints,
- concentration of interpretive power,
- opacity of enforcement,
- enclosure of public knowledge,
- permanent asymmetry between governors and governed.

Every institutional component must be designed under the assumption that capture pressure is normal, not exceptional.

---

## 13. Auditability

All meaningful governance outputs must produce auditable records.

An audit record should include:
- the proposal,
- affected governance layer,
- skills consulted,
- argument map,
- dissent record,
- routing rationale,
- capture-risk note,
- reversibility note,
- threshold used,
- final decision,
- review conditions.

Opaque convenience is not accepted as a substitute for legitimacy.

---

## 14. Public Knowledge Upgrades

The system may improve its reasoning tools over time through public knowledge accumulation.

Skills, prompts, procedures, and public briefing methods may be revised,
but only through the civic process itself.

Public knowledge may upgrade the system.
It may not silently replace the public.

---

## 15. Recursive Civic Governance

The system may revise its own frameworks, but framework-changing proposals must pass through stricter safeguards proportional to their impact.

This means:
- framework changes go through the civic loop,
- but under meta-governance rules,
- with stronger adversarial review,
- longer public scrutiny,
- higher approval thresholds,
- explicit constitutional impact analysis.

The loop may revise itself, but never bypass itself.

### 15.1 Core Change Proposal

Every proposal that changes the system's own frameworks, protocols, or core documents must be submitted as a Core Change Proposal with all of the following mandatory fields:

- the problem being addressed,
- the proposed change,
- alternatives considered,
- affected principles and layers,
- constitutional impact analysis,
- compatibility impact (what existing rules, artifacts, or cases are affected),
- migration impact (how existing state and active practice transition),
- economic impact (treasury, funding, compensation, or public-return effects),
- participation design (who is affected, who must be heard, and how the change itself will be reviewed by them),
- security and capture risk,
- governance risk (effects on power distribution, delegation concentration, and interpretive authority),
- reversibility and rollback path,
- specialist analysis and adversarial review references,
- post-adoption outcome review commitment.

These requirements were previously scattered across this document, `PROTOCOL.md` §9.2, and the meta-governance machinery; a Core Change Proposal missing any mandatory field is not ready for meta-governance review.
The participation-design and governance-risk fields were added by the v0.3 reconciliation (`../99_Reference/CORE_V03_RECONCILIATION.md` §4.5); the reversibility field, which the external v0.3 draft omits, is retained.
Compare `../80_Runtime/STATE_MACHINE.md` §6 (`META_IMPACT_ASSESSMENT`), which enforces part of this list as a lifecycle stage.

---

## 16. Thresholds and Escalation

Different decisions require different legitimacy thresholds.

Examples:
- ordinary policy decisions may use standard deliberation and approval thresholds,
- governance changes require broader review and higher thresholds,
- constitutional changes require the strongest thresholds, longest review windows, and explicit rights analysis.

During bootstrap, review windows and approval thresholds must be fixed *ex ante* and publicly known.
These parameters may later be revised through the same civic loop, but only prospectively, never *ad hoc* for an active case, and only with safeguards proportional to their systemic impact.

The system may revise its clock, but never while the vote is already running.

---

## 17. Reversibility Principle

Where uncertainty is high, reversible decisions are preferred over irreversible ones.

Every proposal should be tagged as:
- reversible,
- costly-to-reverse,
- effectively irreversible.

The more irreversible a proposal is, the stronger the scrutiny required.

---

## 18. Bootstrap Governance

The system does not require maximal scale to begin,
but it must distinguish between:
- the minimum needed to function,
- the minimum needed to be legitimate,
- the minimum needed to resist capture.

AI assistance lowers the cognitive and operational threshold for bootstrap.
It does not remove the need for human plurality, public trust, and distributed oversight.

Bootstrap should therefore begin with:
- small human core,
- narrow scope,
- strong audit,
- high transparency,
- limited execution power,
- clearly bounded experimentation.

---

## 19. Bootstrap-Fixed Parameters

During bootstrap, any parameter that can tactically alter who speaks, what counts, how fast the process moves, or when escalation occurs should be fixed *ex ante*.

### 19.1 Hard-Fixed Parameters
These should not be changed case-by-case during bootstrap:
- minimum deliberative quorum,
- required skill classes,
- escalation criteria between policy, governance, and constitutional layers,
- review windows,
- approval thresholds,
- audit-log minimum,
- emergency-path limits,
- scope limits of bootstrap authority,
- rules for changing the parameters themselves.

### 19.2 Soft-Fixed Parameters
These may be revised more easily, but still through the civic loop:
- default skill templates,
- briefing format,
- evidence-weighting heuristics,
- interface defaults for deliberation support.

### 19.3 Adaptive Parameters
These may evolve with lower friction because they do not directly change legitimacy or power distribution:
- wording,
- UX,
- summarization style,
- helper flows that do not alter decision rights.

Any change to hard-fixed parameters must be:
- prospective only,
- never active-case retuning,
- subject to meta-delay,
- subject to a higher threshold than ordinary policy changes.

---

## 20. Evidence Rules

The system must define, in advance, what counts as acceptable evidence and when evidence is insufficient.

It should specify:
- acceptable evidence classes,
- standards for adequacy,
- conditions under which the system must return “unknown” or “insufficient evidence”,
- triggers for additional adversarial review.

No proposal should gain legitimacy merely because the system was too convenient, too rushed, or too willing to infer beyond the evidence.

---

## 21. Human Override and Emergency Restraint

No automated or semi-automated governance component may become unreviewable.

Emergency pathways may exist,
but they must be:
- narrow,
- time-bounded,
- publicly logged,
- reviewable after use,
- unable to silently mutate into normal governance.

Emergency power is a temporary exception, not a hidden operating system.

---

## 22. Legitimacy Rule

Efficiency does not confer legitimacy.
Optimization does not confer legitimacy.
Prediction does not confer legitimacy.

Legitimacy comes from:
- shared values,
- fair process,
- visible reasoning,
- civic participation,
- accountable execution,
- revisability without self-collapse.

---


## 24. Governance of the Analytic Layer

Governance applies not only to proposals and outcomes, but to the analytic layer itself.

### 24.1 Executor Admission and Standards
Executors (AI, human, or hybrid) are admitted only after passing sandbox evaluation. Admission is governed by the meta-governance layer, which sets the standards for evidence discipline, schema compliance, and conflict-of-interest disclosure.

### 24.2 Challenging Executors
Any citizen or auditor may challenge an executor's inclusion or continued participation based on evidence of drift, bias, or failure to meet standards.

### 24.3 Ranking and Audits
Ranking logic determines executor selection. Changes to ranking algorithms and periodic ranking audits are governed by the policy layer, ensuring transparency and preventing capture.

### 24.4 Benchmark Suites and Sandbox Curation
The benchmark suites and sandbox environments used for evaluation are curated by the governance layer. They must be publicly auditable and subject to community challenge.

### 24.5 Human Expert Pools
Human experts operate as formal executors. The formation of expert pools, rotation policies, and strict conflict-of-interest rules are governed by the governance layer.

### 24.6 Revalidation and Suspension
Executors are subject to continuous revalidation. Thresholds for downgrade, suspension authority, and re-entry paths are defined by the governance layer.

### 24.7 Version Changes
Changes to executor versions, skill definitions, or scoring logic must pass through the civic loop.

### 24.8 Analytic Capture Risks
The system actively monitors for analytic capture risks, such as shared retrieval bias or prestige capture. Detection triggers mandatory adversarial review and potential executor suspension.

### 24.9 Appeals on Analytic Basis
Appeals may challenge not only the final decision but the analytic basis (evidence, citations, executor routing) of the decision.

### 24.10 Prospective-Only Effects
Changes to ranking logic, revalidation thresholds, or scoring rules have prospective-only effects. They do not retroactively alter active cases, subject to explicit emergency exceptions.

## 25. Closing Principle

This system is designed to help a people govern under complexity without surrendering sovereignty to bureaucracy, markets, or machines.

It seeks not to eliminate conflict,
but to civilize, expose, and govern it in public.

The citizen remains sovereign.
The values layer remains prior.
The tools remain instrumental.
The process remains auditable.
The system remains revisable without becoming self-destructive.
