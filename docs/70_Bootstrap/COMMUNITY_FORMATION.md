# COMMUNITY_FORMATION

> **Note:** This document has been updated to reflect the normative rules in [SYSTEM_PATCH_v2](../99_Reference/SYSTEM_PATCH_v2.md).

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how the system forms, attracts, and stabilizes an initial community.

Its purpose is to address a problem prior to governance:
a civic system is useless if no real group wants to use it.

The system must therefore answer:
- who joins first,
- why they join,
- what problem they are trying to solve,
- why they would trust advisory output from an unproven system,
- what would make them return for a second cycle,
- how a small founding circle becomes a real civic body rather than a self-referential project.

This document complements:
- `MINIMUM_VIABLE_PNYX.md`
- `PROTOTYPE_PLAN.md`
- `POLITICAL_ECONOMY.md`
- `CONSTITUTIONAL_BOOTSTRAP.md`
- `FUNDING_MODEL.md`
- `PACKET_FORMAT.md`

Community formation is not marketing decoration.
It is part of the constitutional bootstrap problem.

---

## 2. Core Principle

Communities do not form around constitutional prose.
They form around recurring pain, visible usefulness, and growing trust.

The first users will not join because the specification is elegant.
They will join if the system helps them:
- understand a live issue,
- reduce thread chaos,
- surface disagreement honestly,
- make options clearer,
- preserve minority objections,
- reduce coordination waste,
- create outputs better than their current informal process.

The first community must therefore be built around real utility before broad legitimacy claims.

---

## 3. What Community Formation Is

Community formation is the process by which a bounded group moves from:
- curiosity,
- to first use,
- to repeated use,
- to shared norms around packet interpretation, challenge, and review,
- to understanding how citizens challenge analytic basis,
- to forming and rotating human expert pools,
- to community-auditing benchmark suites,
- to establishing sourcing norms as part of civic culture,
- to recognizing that legitimacy depends on public inspectability rather than hidden expert trust,
- to enough trust that the civic loop is treated as a meaningful common process.

This is not the same as:
- audience growth,
- follower count,
- content reach,
- speculative hype,
- founder fandom,
- or general ideological sympathy.

The system does not need a crowd first.
It needs a real group with a real problem.

---

## 4. First-Adopter Profile

The best first adopters are likely to be groups that already experience recurring coordination pain.

Examples may include:
- local associations,
- cooperatives,
- volunteer groups,
- advocacy working groups,
- neighborhood assemblies,
- issue-based civic communities,
- unions or member bodies using advisory deliberation support,
- municipal or quasi-municipal experimental groups where legally appropriate.

The best first-adopter group usually has:
- recurring proposals,
- visible disagreement,
- weak current documentation quality,
- enough trust to try a new process,
- enough friction that improvement is valuable,
- enough continuity to run multiple cycles.

The wrong first adopters are groups selected mainly for prestige, publicity, or symbolic importance.

---

## 5. Entry Problem

The first question most people ask is not:
- Is this constitutionally elegant?

It is:
- Why should I use this instead of chat threads, meetings, documents, or existing civic channels?

The system must therefore provide one or more initial answers such as:
- it produces clearer issue packets than your current process,
- it preserves disagreement without meeting chaos,
- it helps groups compare options without losing minority concerns,
- it gives a transparent trace of why a recommendation was shaped the way it was,
- it makes repeated proposal cycles less exhausting.

If the first answer is only philosophical, adoption will remain abstract.

---

## 6. Trust Ladder

Trust should be expected to grow in stages.

### 6.1 Curiosity
People are willing to look at one output.

### 6.2 Procedural Tolerance
People are willing to let one issue pass through the loop.

### 6.3 Comparative Trust
People think the output was more useful than the ordinary process.

### 6.4 Challenge Trust
People believe criticism can actually change or contest the packet.

### 6.5 Reuse Trust
People voluntarily return for another cycle.

### 6.6 Shared Process Trust
The group begins to treat the loop as a common civic instrument rather than a founder performance.

A bootstrap system should track which rung it has reached.
It must not claim higher trust than it has earned.

---

## 7. First Value Proposition

The first value proposition should be narrow and concrete.

Good early value propositions include:
- better issue briefing,
- cleaner proposal comparison,
- visible disagreement mapping,
- minority-view preservation,
- auditable revision history,
- lower coordination fatigue,
- better memory across cycles.

Bad early value propositions include:
- "the future of democracy,"
- "AI governance revolution,"
- "replacement for the political system,"
- "fully sovereign civic runtime."

The system must earn the right to say bigger things later.

---

## 8. First Use Cases

The first use cases should have the following properties:

- bounded scope,
- recurring relevance,
- moderate disagreement,
- real consequences but not catastrophic consequences,
- comparison with existing process is possible,
- challenge and revision are meaningful,
- community members can tell whether the packet helped.

Strong first use cases may include:
- prioritizing local improvements,
- cooperative policy draft review,
- neighborhood issue comparison,
- volunteer association proposals,
- public comment synthesis for a bounded community,
- structured analysis of candidate actions in a civic project.

Weak first use cases include:
- constitutional redesign,
- nationwide emergency policy,
- highly polarized symbolic issues,
- high-stakes irreversible disputes in the first cycle.

---

## 9. Community Formation Path

A recommended early path is:

1. identify a bounded real group,
2. identify one recurring proposal problem,
3. run one advisory cycle,
4. publish the packet and challenge path,
5. compare with the group's normal process,
6. collect structured feedback,
7. revise the runtime and packet surface,
8. repeat with the same group,
9. expand only after repeated use.

The point is not one impressive demonstration.
The point is repeated usefulness.

---

## 10. Advisory-First Posture

The system should begin as advisory-first.

This is important because:
- it lowers fear,
- reduces legitimacy overclaim,
- makes failure survivable,
- allows comparison with current process,
- makes challenge more acceptable,
- keeps trust growth empirical.

An advisory packet that is actually used in discussion is stronger evidence than a binding system nobody trusts.

---

## 11. Community Formation and Skepticism

A healthy first community should include:
- one or more supportive users,
- one or more skeptical users,
- at least one likely challenger,
- people with direct stake in the issue,
- at least one participant who would normally be underserved by thread chaos or meeting overload.

The system should not try to eliminate skepticism early.
Skepticism is one of the first tests of whether the challenge and audit surfaces mean anything.

---

## 12. Founder Role Boundaries

Founders may initiate community formation.
They must not confuse initiation with legitimacy.

Founders may:
- recruit an initial pilot group,
- explain the tool,
- support onboarding,
- collect feedback,
- operate the first cycles visibly.

Founders may not:
- pretend early adoption equals broad legitimacy,
- suppress challenge because trust is fragile,
- quietly rewrite unfavorable outputs,
- recruit only admirers,
- treat loyalty as evidence of fit.

A founder-built community can still become real,
but only if the founder role becomes progressively less central.

---

## 13. Community Fit Criteria

A candidate pilot community is a strong fit if:
- it has a recurring decision or proposal problem,
- existing process quality is visibly poor or tiring,
- participants care enough to compare outcomes,
- the group can survive one failed experiment,
- there is some cultural tolerance for process iteration,
- at least part of the community is willing to challenge outputs rather than passively consume them.

A weak fit community is one where:
- issues are purely performative,
- participation is entirely symbolic,
- trust is too low for any common process,
- conflict is so explosive that a first failure destroys future use,
- nobody has time to review packets or submit challenge.

---

## 14. Community Formation Metrics

The system should track community formation signals such as:
- first-cycle participation count,
- repeat-cycle participation,
- number of real proposals submitted,
- number of real challenges submitted,
- number of packet revisions triggered,
- participant-reported usefulness,
- participant-reported trust in challengeability,
- proportion of discussion that actually references the packet,
- whether the group requests another cycle.

These are more meaningful than passive page views or social-media approval.

---

## 15. Participation Inequality in Formation

Community formation must assume that the same inequalities described in `POLITICAL_ECONOMY.md` are already active.

This means:
- early users may be more educated or time-rich,
- technical founders may over-shape norms,
- stronger communicators may dominate interpretation,
- low-trust users may remain observers rather than participants.

The system should therefore deliberately lower entry burden through:
- short packet summaries,
- plain-language onboarding,
- small first issue scope,
- visible challenge paths,
- local facilitation where possible,
- explicit acknowledgment of who is likely missing.

The system cannot solve inequality at entry.
But it can stop pretending it is absent.

---

## 16. Community Intermediaries

In many cases the system will need intermediaries before it reaches broad direct use.

Possible intermediaries include:
- local facilitators,
- trusted organizers,
- translators,
- civic educators,
- community hosts,
- issue-specific explainers,
- operators with explicit public role boundaries.

Intermediaries are not automatically bad.
They are risky when they become irreplaceable or invisible.

The system should therefore prefer:
- visible intermediary roles,
- replaceable facilitation,
- public packet access independent of any one host,
- challenge paths not monopolized by facilitators.

---

## 17. Why People Return

A first-use system becomes a community instrument only when people return.

People are likely to return if:
- the packet was genuinely useful,
- disagreements were represented fairly,
- challenge changed something real,
- the system reduced exhaustion,
- the process was not humiliating or manipulative,
- operator interventions were visible,
- the next cycle felt easier rather than heavier.

People are unlikely to return if:
- the packet was jargon-heavy,
- the process mainly served founders,
- challenge felt theatrical,
- the loop increased work without increasing clarity,
- the output was politically bland or obviously distorted,
- the same insiders controlled all meaning.

Repeat use is the first real test of community formation.

---

## 18. Community Expansion

Expansion should happen only after repeated successful cycles.

Expansion may include:
- a second issue domain,
- a second group with similar needs,
- a second locale,
- a shared commons layer across multiple groups,
- broader challenge participation,
- stronger local stewardship.

Expansion should not happen merely because:
- the documents are complete,
- one packet looked good,
- social attention rises,
- founders become impatient,
- a funder wants scale.

Scale before community fit is acceleration into irrelevance.

---

## 19. Legitimacy Path

The path to legitimacy should be treated as staged.

### Stage 1 — Useful Advisory Tool
The system helps a real group think better.

### Stage 2 — Repeated Civic Instrument
The same group uses it across multiple cycles.

### Stage 3 — Trusted Community Process
Participants expect packets, challenge, and review as part of normal issue handling.

### Stage 4 — Federated Recognition
Other groups adopt it because they have seen repeated usefulness, not because founders claimed legitimacy.

The system should not jump rhetorical stages.

---

## 20. Community Formation Risks

Key risks include:
- founder-centered adoption,
- community selected for friendliness rather than fit,
- hype preceding usefulness,
- packet complexity exceeding community patience,
- challenge pathways that only insiders use,
- false appearance of participation,
- users treating the system as a content generator rather than a civic process,
- no second-cycle adoption,
- burnout in community hosts,
- symbolic participation without real issue relevance.

These risks should be treated as design signals, not marketing obstacles.

---

## 21. Bootstrap Narrative Rules

The public narrative around early adoption should follow strict limits.

The system should say:
- this is a bounded prototype,
- this is advisory,
- this is being tested on real issues,
- this is expected to fail and improve,
- this is not yet proof of broad legitimacy.

The system should not say:
- democracy solved,
- governance captured safely,
- objective civic intelligence achieved,
- nationwide readiness,
- constitutional authority earned.

Narrative inflation is one of the fastest ways to destroy trust before it exists.

---

## 22. Community Artifacts

A healthy community formation process should produce:
- pilot invitation or onboarding text,
- issue-domain definition,
- packet examples,
- challenge examples,
- participant feedback summaries,
- operator intervention logs,
- repeat-cycle evidence,
- community retrospectives.

These artifacts help distinguish actual adoption from founder impression.

---

## 23. Community Failure Conditions

The community formation process should be treated as failed or paused if:
- no real group wants a second cycle,
- participation remains almost entirely founder-driven,
- challenge paths remain unused due to confusion,
- packet usefulness is repeatedly low,
- no materially affected participants engage,
- skepticism is filtered out instead of integrated,
- the process is admired rhetorically but not used practically,
- the system attracts spectators but not civic users.

A community system with no real community should stop pretending it exists.

## 25. Closing Principle

A civic system is not born when its rules are written.
It is born when a real group uses it twice.

The first political task of the system is therefore not scale.
It is to become usefully real for one community with one recurring problem.

Only then does governance stop being a document set
and start becoming a public relationship.
