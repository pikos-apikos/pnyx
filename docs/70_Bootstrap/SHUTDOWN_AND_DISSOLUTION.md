# SHUTDOWN_AND_DISSOLUTION

> **Note:** This document has been updated to reflect the normative rules in [SYSTEM_PATCH_v2](../99_Reference/SYSTEM_PATCH_v2.md).

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how the system pauses, shuts down, dissolves, or transfers stewardship when continuation is no longer justified or no longer possible.

Its purpose is to prevent the project from assuming that once started it must continue indefinitely.
A civic system that can define emergency limits, bootstrap debt, and challenge rights must also define:
- when it should stop,
- how it should stop,
- who may decide,
- what happens to funds,
- what happens to records,
- what happens to public IP,
- what happens to community commitments,
- and how dissolution itself avoids becoming a capture event.

This document complements:
- `BOOTSTRAP_REALITY_CHECK.md`

Shutdown is not merely administrative.
It is part of constitutional honesty.

## 2. Core Principle

The system may stop.

Stopping may be:
- responsible,
- necessary,
- protective,
- honest,
- and preferable to institutional theater.

Continuation is justified only when the system still produces enough civic value, trust, and learning to warrant its ongoing cost and concentration risks.

The system must reject three false assumptions:
- that every project should continue because effort was already spent,
- that partial adoption is always proof of future legitimacy,
- that founders are morally obliged to keep the system alive at any cost.

The right to stop is part of anti-capture.

---

## 3. What Shutdown Means

Shutdown is an umbrella term covering different exit states.

### 3.1 Pause
A temporary suspension of active cycles while preserving the possibility of restart.

### 3.2 Controlled Wind-Down
A bounded process that closes active operations, settles commitments, and archives the system without immediate deletion.

### 3.3 Dissolution
A formal end of the operating civic system as a live governance runtime.

### 3.4 Stewardship Transfer
A handoff of some or all components to another clearly declared steward or community.

### 3.5 Subsystem Retirement
The ending of a major subsystem while the broader project continues.

The system should distinguish these states rather than using "shutdown" as a vague label.

---

## 4. Why Shutdown Must Exist

A shutdown framework is required because the following are possible:
- the community never forms,
- the prototype never becomes useful,
- challenge paths remain decorative,
- founders burn out,
- budget becomes unsustainable,
- trust collapses,
- capture occurs,
- legal context changes,
- operator concentration becomes unacceptable,
- the system proves too complex for its actual use value.

A governance project with no theory of ending quietly assumes its own necessity.
That is already a constitutional hazard.

---

## 5. Shutdown Triggers

Shutdown or pause must be considered when one or more of the following are true:

### 5.1 Adoption Failure
- no real community completes a second cycle,
- users do not return,
- packets are not used in actual discussion,
- the system remains founder-performed rather than community-used.

### 5.2 Procedural Failure
- silent interventions recur,
- challenge paths are nominal only,
- audit surfaces become unreadable,
- active-case retuning occurs,
- key invariants repeatedly fail,
- concentrated executor capture,
- ranking corruption,
- benchmark corruption,
- evidence corruption,
- mass miscalibration,
- collapse of trusted human pools,
- inability to validate current law.

### 5.3 Economic Failure
- funding cannot sustain another bounded cycle,
- reserves are exhausted,
- donor concentration becomes structurally unacceptable,
- treasury cannot support challenge and continuity at the minimum floor.

### 5.4 Organizational Failure
- founding team burns out,
- irreplaceable roles remain concentrated,
- no realistic succession exists,
- community hosts withdraw.

### 5.5 Constitutional Failure
- bootstrap debt cannot be meaningfully reduced,
- public trust collapses,
- the system becomes theater,
- the project's own anti-capture rules are no longer credible.

### 5.6 Legal or Safety Failure
- legal risk exceeds prototype scope,
- serious harm has been caused and cannot be responsibly contained,
- public records or user obligations cannot be maintained safely.

A trigger does not always require immediate dissolution.
It does require explicit review.

---

## 6. Shutdown Decision Classes

Shutdown decisions should be classified by severity.

### 6.1 Operational Pause Decision
For temporary interruption while preserving possible restart.

### 6.2 Controlled Wind-Down Decision
For ending current operations without immediately dissolving stewardship.

### 6.3 Full Dissolution Decision
For ending the civic runtime and treasury operations as a live system.

### 6.4 Transfer Decision
For handing stewardship to another body or community.

Each class requires different review, timing, and closure requirements.

---

## 7. Who May Initiate Shutdown Review

Shutdown review may be initiated by:
- founders or stewards,
- operators,
- treasury or audit stewards,
- a declared review body,
- a challenge pathway when constitutional failure is alleged,
- the pilot community where applicable,
- a pre-declared minimum threshold of participants or members where such a body exists.

Initiation is not the same as execution.
The process must remain visible and bounded.

---

## 8. Shutdown Review Questions

Every shutdown review should answer:

- What exactly is failing?
- Is the failure temporary, structural, or unknown?
- Can the system be simplified instead of stopped?
- Is a pause enough?
- Is the founding team still capable of another cycle?
- Is the community still willing to use the system?
- Is the treasury still viable?
- Are records and obligations still maintainable?
- Would continuation create more theater than value?
- Is transfer realistic?

A shutdown review must not ask only "can we keep going?"
It must ask "should we keep going?"

---

## 9. Shutdown States for Active Cases

When pause or shutdown begins, active cases must be explicitly handled.

Each active proposal, challenge, or pilot artifact must be marked as:
- completed before shutdown,
- frozen pending restart,
- withdrawn due to shutdown,
- archived as incomplete,
- transferred to successor stewardship if applicable.

No active case may be silently abandoned.

The final public state of each case must be visible.

---

## 10. Treasury During Shutdown

The treasury must have a shutdown posture.

At minimum, shutdown must specify:
- freeze rules for new obligations,
- allowed continuity spending,
- reserve protection rules,
- how outstanding grants or bounties are handled,
- what happens to emergency reserves,
- what happens to challenge funding,
- final reconciliation schedule,
- disposition of remaining funds,
- conditions for transfer versus dissolution.

Treasury shutdown must remain auditable and challengeable.

---

## 11. Remaining Funds

Remaining funds should be disposed of according to pre-declared hierarchy consistent with funding restrictions and public-return obligations.

Possible disposition paths include:
- refund where legally and operationally appropriate,
- transfer to a successor civic body,
- transfer to designated commons maintenance,
- transfer to challenge or archival stewardship,
- return to restricted-purpose pools,
- legally required dissolution handling.

Remaining funds must not:
- become founder compensation by default,
- be redirected informally,
- disappear into administrative ambiguity,
- be captured by the last operator standing.

---

## 12. Public IP During Shutdown

Public IP and commons assets require explicit shutdown handling.

Shutdown must specify:
- who remains steward of commons-core artifacts,
- whether stewardship transfers,
- which repositories or artifacts remain public,
- what happens to licensing posture,
- how documentation is preserved,
- whether public IP revenue streams stop or transfer,
- whether maintenance enters archival mode.

Public IP produced through civic means must not silently vanish or be enclosed during dissolution.

---

## 13. Records, Audit, and Archival Duties

A shutdown process must preserve the public memory of the system.

At minimum, it must define:
- what audit records remain available,
- what packet records remain available,
- what operator intervention logs remain available,
- what treasury records remain available,
- what challenge histories remain available,
- how read-only archival access will work,
- what privacy protections remain in force after shutdown,
- who maintains the archive.

The system must prefer archival continuity over erasure,
except where lawful privacy obligations require redaction or deletion.

---

## 14. Privacy and Deletion Boundaries

Shutdown does not dissolve privacy obligations.

The project must define:
- what personal or sensitive data may be deleted,
- what must be retained for public accountability,
- how redaction is handled,
- how archival records preserve truth without violating legitimate confidentiality,
- who is responsible for final privacy review.

"Archiving everything" is not automatically legitimate.
"Deleting everything" is not automatically honest.

---

## 15. Community Obligations During Shutdown

If a real community has been using the system, shutdown must respect that relationship.

This includes:
- public explanation of why shutdown is happening,
- status of current and prior outputs,
- whether another cycle will occur,
- whether successor stewardship exists,
- what remains available to participants,
- where records can still be found,
- whether community feedback is part of final review.

A civic system should not disappear in the night.

---

## 16. Pause Conditions

A pause is appropriate when:
- the team needs reduction or recovery,
- funding exists but confidence is temporarily low,
- a pilot community wants time before another cycle,
- evaluation debt has become too high,
- a subsystem needs repair,
- legal or technical clarification is needed before continuing.

A pause must specify:
- start date,
- expected review date,
- what remains active,
- what is frozen,
- what restart conditions apply,
- who is responsible during pause.

A pause without review date is drift disguised as caution.

---

## 17. Controlled Wind-Down

Controlled wind-down is appropriate when:
- continuation is not justified soon,
- there are still obligations to complete,
- some treasury actions must settle,
- archival work remains,
- transfer is uncertain but possible.

A wind-down should define:
- closure timeline,
- no-new-initiatives rule,
- final spending policy,
- final archival duties,
- final challenge window,
- final review artifact,
- final treasury reconciliation.

Wind-down is not indefinite limbo.

---

## 18. Dissolution

Dissolution is appropriate when:
- the system no longer has a real user community,
- continuation would be mostly performative,
- resources are insufficient for honest operation,
- concentration is unacceptably persistent,
- legal or trust failure is severe,
- no plausible recovery path exists.

Dissolution should produce:
- a dissolution record,
- final treasury reconciliation,
- archive disposition,
- public IP disposition,
- privacy disposition,
- successor statement if any,
- bootstrap debt final status,
- final lessons-learned report.

Dissolution is an ending, not just inactivity.

---

## 19. Stewardship Transfer

Transfer is appropriate only when:
- the successor body is real,
- responsibilities are explicit,
- archives can remain available,
- treasury obligations are legally and operationally transferable,
- public IP stewardship is preserved,
- participants can see who now holds what responsibilities.

A vague "someone else will keep it alive" is not stewardship transfer.

Transfer must record:
- receiving steward,
- transferred assets,
- transferred obligations,
- transferred records,
- transfer limits,
- effective date,
- public notice.

---

## 20. Subsystem Retirement

Sometimes the right move is not shutting down the whole system,
but retiring a subsystem.

Examples:
- treasury remains, but a pilot runtime ends,
- archives remain, but active proposal intake stops,
- skill commons remain, but packet generation service pauses,
- public IP maintenance continues, but civic loop operation ends.

Subsystem retirement must still be:
- explicit,
- auditable,
- challengeable where relevant,
- non-silent,
- linked to the overall shutdown posture.

---

## 21. Shutdown Governance Boundaries

Shutdown must not become a power grab.

No actor may use shutdown to:
- seize public IP,
- suppress unfavorable archives,
- redirect funds privately,
- erase challenge history,
- reframe failure as success without evidence,
- renew their own control by "temporary" continuation,
- eliminate critics while preserving operator privilege.

The end of a system is one of the highest-risk capture moments.

---

## 22. Shutdown Audit Artifacts

A serious shutdown should produce, at minimum:
- `ShutdownReviewRecord`
- `ShutdownDecisionRecord`
- `ActiveCaseDispositionLog`
- `TreasuryDispositionRecord`
- `ArchivePlan`
- `PublicIPDispositionRecord`
- `PrivacyDispositionRecord`
- `FinalRetrospective`
- `SuccessorOrDissolutionNotice`

Without these artifacts, shutdown becomes rumor.

---

## 23. Final Retrospective

Every wind-down or dissolution should produce a final retrospective.

It should answer:
- what the system actually accomplished,
- what failed,
- what remained untested,
- what should not be repeated,
- what components were genuinely useful,
- what proved too heavy,
- what community signals were real,
- what assumptions failed,
- whether a future restart should reuse any part.

This retrospective should be public where lawful and appropriate.

The point is not self-justification.
It is honest inheritance.

---

## 24. Restart Conditions

A paused or dissolved system should define whether restart is allowed.

If restart is allowed, it should answer:
- what artifacts remain reusable,
- what must be revalidated,
- what debts remain open,
- whether the same founders or operators may restart it,
- whether a new community or funding base is required,
- whether prior failures impose stronger constraints.

A restart must not pretend the previous shutdown never happened.

---

## 25. Failure Is Not Shame

The project should explicitly adopt the principle that shutdown can be a success of honesty.

A civic prototype that:
- surfaced its own limits,
- protected community records,
- preserved public value,
- avoided false legitimacy,
- and stopped before becoming theater,

has succeeded more than a project that lingers symbolically.

The constitutional danger is not stopping.
It is continuing after meaningful failure.

---

## 26. Bootstrap-Specific Shutdown Rules

During bootstrap, shutdown rules should be simpler and stricter.

Bootstrap should prefer:
- fast explicit pause over vague inactivity,
- strong archival duty,
- conservative treasury handling,
- clear public explanation,
- minimal unresolved obligations,
- visible founder responsibility.

Bootstrap should avoid:
- trying to preserve every subsystem,
- rhetorical refusal to admit failure,
- using "prototype" as excuse for silent disappearance.

Bootstrap silence is one of the fastest ways to destroy future trust.

## 28. Closing Principle

A democratic project should know how to end without lying.

It should be able to say:
- this helped,
- this failed,
- this remains useful,
- this should be archived,
- this should be transferred,
- this should stop here.

Only a system that can end honestly has any chance of governing honestly.
